The Network Comes Later

Created on August 25, 2026

2026   ·   human-interaction   coordination   trust   networks   proof-of-work   ·   essays

中文

TL;DR — Meaningful professional relationships are not built by pursuing access to people. They emerge when shared problems make ability visible, useful work reaches the right people, and repeated evidence compounds into trust. Build something worth coordinating around; the network comes later.

I have become increasingly suspicious of the way people talk about networking. The usual advice begins with a list of people: Who should I meet? Who matters in this field? How can I get introduced? What should I say so that they remember me? Once those questions become the starting point, it is easy to fall into a familiar pattern—adding someone on WeChat after a conference, sending a respected researcher a vague note about “learning from them,” or asking for collaboration before there is anything concrete to collaborate on. The exchange may be polite, but it contains almost no information. The other person still does not know what you care about, what you can do, how you think, or whether working with you would move anything forward. You have requested a relationship before giving that relationship a reason to exist.

My instinct now is almost the opposite: do not begin with the relationship. Begin with something real. Begin with a problem you are willing to work on, a claim you are willing to test, a tool you are willing to build, or a question that can survive contact with evidence. Meaningful professional relationships tend to appear downstream of those commitments. The strongest ones are rarely built by two people staring at each other and trying to manufacture affinity. They are built by two people looking in the same direction, noticing the same part of the world, and discovering that each has something useful to contribute to the other’s understanding of it.

Shared reality before connection

Imagine two researchers studying long-running agents. One believes the central bottleneck is memory; the other believes it is evaluation. They do not need to agree in order to have a meaningful relationship. Their disagreement already has structure because both are responding to the same object. They can compare evidence, expose assumptions, design interventions, and learn from the places where their models diverge. The conversation does not need an artificial opening line, because the problem itself generates the conversation. What begins as “I want to know this person” is replaced by something more grounded: “We are both trying to understand this thing.” That shift is small in language but enormous in consequence.

This is why serious intellectual communities often form around questions rather than personalities. Researchers recognize one another while repeatedly colliding with the same bottleneck. Builders begin exchanging tools because their problems touch. People with different beliefs about the same phenomenon argue, collaborate, and gradually sharpen one another’s thinking. The relationship becomes a by-product of sustained attention to shared reality. It is not a prize granted before the work begins, and it is not a separate social layer floating above the work. It is part of the coordination that becomes necessary once multiple people care enough about the same thing to act on it.

None of this means that every human interaction should become instrumental, or that friendship and curiosity require a measurable output. That would reproduce the same mistake in a colder form. The point is not “never talk to people.” It is to avoid manufactured networking: pursuing contact as if contact itself were progress, treating people as resources, or attempting to create intimacy without shared substance. A better principle is to spend less energy asking how to know a particular person and more energy asking what you could work on that would give both of you a natural reason to speak.

Proof before proximity

The difficulty is that ability is invisible until it leaves evidence. You may believe that you understand agents, benchmarks, or research methodology; you may believe that you have unusual taste or the capacity to do difficult work. But all of this is latent. From the outside, potential cannot be inspected directly. Other people can only update their model of you through what becomes observable: the code you write, the experiments you run, the failures you identify, the essays you publish, the questions you formulate, and the way you respond when the first approach does not work.

A repository, benchmark, dataset, pull request, careful analysis, or surprising negative result is therefore more than an output. It is evidence. Each piece of work converts an uncheckable claim about ability into something another person can examine. Repeated evidence makes competence legible. It allows people to infer what kinds of problems you notice, whether you distinguish confidence from speculation, whether you finish what you start, and whether interacting with you tends to move a problem forward. Trust does not emerge from how confidently you describe yourself. It emerges as other people accumulate reasons to believe that your judgment and execution are reliable.

This is why ten hours spent making something real can be more socially valuable than ten hours spent trying to meet people. Good work speaks on your behalf, but more importantly, it lowers the cost of understanding you. Instead of asking someone to imagine what you might be capable of, you give them an object through which they can see how you think. The interaction changes from “Please give me some of your attention” to “I have already moved this problem forward; here is what I found, and here is the part that remains unresolved.” You are no longer requesting proximity in the hope that value might later appear. You are bringing evidence of value into the conversation from the beginning.

The same logic explains why doing most of the work yourself is such a strong signal. It shows that you are not waiting for another person to supply momentum. If someone offers you a useful idea, an introduction, or ten minutes of expert judgment, there is evidence that you can absorb that leverage and multiply it. This makes helping you less like adopting an unfinished obligation and more like placing a small amount of force at a point where the system is already moving.

Ask after doing the work

Asking for help too early often produces weak interactions because the request quietly transfers most of the cognitive burden to the other person. A question like “How should I pursue this research direction?” requires the expert to define the problem, map the search space, generate hypotheses, and decide what you should do next. The apparent humility of the question conceals how much work it demands. In contrast, suppose you have read the relevant literature, built a prototype, tried three approaches, eliminated two explanations, and isolated the uncertainty that remains. You can now ask: “The evidence is consistent with A or B, but my current experiment cannot distinguish them. What intervention would be most informative?” That is a completely different object.

At that point, the expert is not being asked to perform your work. They are applying a small amount of specialized judgment precisely where their comparative advantage is greatest. A hundred hours of independent effort can be unlocked by ten minutes of insight. The exchange is valuable to you, but it is also easier and more interesting for the other person because they are responding to a developed problem rather than an empty space. The result is not merely an answer. It is evidence that you know how to formulate uncertainty, respect another person’s time, and turn advice into progress.

This does not mean waiting until the work is perfect or refusing help as a matter of pride. The goal is to reduce uncertainty as far as you reasonably can, state clearly what you have tried, and expose the actual bottleneck. High-quality help becomes possible when the request is concrete, bounded, and directed toward something the other person is unusually equipped to see. The best pattern is not independence forever. It is independent motion followed by precise coordination.

Distribution is part of the work

There is an opposite romantic error among builders: the belief that if the work is good enough, the right people will inevitably discover it. Often they will not. Value creation and value discovery are different problems. An excellent project can remain invisible in a repository with three stars; a useful benchmark can fail to reach the teams who need it; an important idea can disappear because it never entered the channels through which relevant people allocate attention. Building is necessary, but the existence of value does not guarantee its discovery.

Distribution must therefore be treated as part of the work. In this context, distribution is not self-promotion and should not be optimized for maximum exposure. It is an information-routing problem: what have I created, who would genuinely find it useful, and through which trusted channels can it reach them? Five views from researchers actively working on the same bottleneck may be worth more than ten thousand random impressions. A message in the right research group, a link sent to one builder with an adjacent problem, or a recommendation from a trusted colleague may be enough to create the first meaningful edge.

This changes how outreach is written. “I admire your work and would love to connect” requests a relationship while offering little evidence about why the interaction should continue. “I built a benchmark related to the failure mode in your recent paper, and one result made me reconsider your explanation” does something else: it routes potentially relevant value. The first message asks the recipient to invent the substance of the exchange. The second arrives with a shared object already in view. Even if no relationship follows, the interaction can still be worthwhile because the information itself has a reason to travel.

Early work often needs this kind of deliberate network seeding. Before a community exists around an idea, someone must create the first edges. You show the work to a small number of people who genuinely face the problem. One person tries it, another identifies a flaw, a third forwards it to a colleague, and someone else opens an issue or proposes an extension. At first, you are pushing the work toward people. If the object proves useful, the direction gradually reverses: people begin finding the work, bringing in other people, and creating connections that no longer depend on your direct effort.

At that point, the work begins to function as a coordination attractor—something that concentrates attention, ability, and action that would otherwise remain scattered. A benchmark gives different research groups a common measurement target. An open-source project gives strangers a shared surface on which to contribute. A serious scientific question can organize communities for decades, even when many of the participants never meet. The attractor matters not merely because it is interesting, but because it gives people a reason to act in relation to one another.

This suggests a more ambitious alternative to ordinary networking. Instead of asking, “How can I meet more impressive people?” ask, “What could I create that thoughtful people would naturally want to engage with?” Building relationships one by one keeps you at the center of every edge; each connection must be initiated, maintained, and refreshed. Creating an object worth coordinating around changes the topology. The project becomes a meeting place, the question becomes a shared language, and people begin discovering the work, one another, and eventually you through the work itself.

From a trust seed to a capability map

When two strangers have one serious exchange, the most important result is not that they have spoken once. It is that each person begins forming a compressed model of the other. The model may initially contain only a few facts: what this person works on, what they appear to understand, whether their judgment was useful, and whether they followed through. Those facts are enough to plant a trust seed. Later, when a related problem appears, the person may be retrieved from memory. An evaluation question appears, and you remember the person who thinks clearly about evaluation. A project needs a particular skill, and you remember who has already demonstrated it.

This is a deeper threshold than exchanging contact details. A relationship begins to matter when each person enters the other’s capability map—when both have an accurate enough model of what the other is good for, where their judgment is reliable, and when involving them would improve the work. Repeated interactions sharpen that model. “I have met this person” becomes “I can ask this person about this topic.” Later it becomes “I would trust this person with this part of the work.” Eventually it may become “If I see the right opportunity, I should bring this person in.”

Introductions have value for exactly this reason. A strong introduction is not simply access to someone else’s attention; it is transferred trust. The person making the introduction spends a small amount of their own reputation on the claim that the connection is worth making. That claim becomes credible when it is grounded in evidence, especially evidence produced through shared work. Working together reveals far more than a polished self-description ever could: how someone behaves under ambiguity, responds to failure, communicates constraints, keeps commitments, and changes their mind when the evidence changes.

Over time, a real network becomes much more than a collection of contacts. It becomes a distributed index of capability and trust. Who understands this problem? Who has good judgment here? Who can execute under uncertainty? Who should be invited into this opportunity? A mature network answers these questions continuously, routing new problems and opportunities toward people who have become legible through prior action. Its value lies less in how many nodes it contains than in how accurately those nodes understand one another.

One of the most important forms of social capital, then, is not being widely known. It is being correctly indexed. You want the right people to have an accurate model of what you are unusually good at. The cleanest way to create that model is not to repeat a description of yourself until it becomes familiar. It is to leave enough evidence that the description becomes unnecessary.

The network comes later

The argument is not “do not socialize.” It is “do not mistake social motion for meaningful progress.” Work on things worth knowing you for. Make your ability legible through evidence. Push a problem as far as you can before asking for leverage. Route what you create toward the people for whom it is genuinely relevant. Let shared problems generate conversations, let useful interactions generate trust, and let trust route future collaborations, introductions, and opportunities.

Do not create conversations merely to acquire relationships. Let relationships emerge while advancing problems that matter. And instead of spending all your energy trying to meet impressive people, create something that impressive people would have a reason to join. The strongest network is often not the one you assemble directly. It is the one that forms around work that gives people a reason to find one another.

Create value. Make it discoverable. Give people a surface on which to coordinate. The network comes later.
