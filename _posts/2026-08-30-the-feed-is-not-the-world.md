> **TL;DR —** The internet does not show us a random sample of reality. What reaches us has already been selected, and the selection compounds before the mind turns observation into a belief about the world. Every post may be true, yet the world assembled from those truths can still be deeply unrepresentative.

## The feeling that everyone else is ahead

I have started to distrust the feeling that everyone else is doing better.

It appears in different forms depending on where I look. On one platform, it is the feeling that everyone preparing for a graduate entrance exam has been admitted. Somewhere else, it becomes the impression that every researcher is publishing papers, every startup is raising money, and every competitor is winning prizes. On platforms built around personal stories, it can feel as if ordinary relationships barely exist anymore, only betrayal, humiliation, conflict, and resentment.

We usually explain these impressions one at a time. Exam anxiety becomes comparison. Career anxiety becomes survivorship bias. Gender hostility becomes polarization. None of those explanations is completely wrong, but taken together they point to a broader question: what happens when a collection of true but selected events becomes the material from which we construct a picture of reality?

My working hypothesis is simple. What reaches us has already been selected. Some people speak more readily than others, some experiences are easier to display, and some kinds of content travel farther once they enter a platform. I do not want to assume that these mechanisms always produce the same outcome. I want to understand what happens when they operate in sequence.

## The people who disappear first

Imagine 100 people preparing for an exam. Perhaps 20 of them eventually succeed, while 80 do not reach the outcome they wanted. You will never encounter all 100 of them online. The successful student has an obvious announcement, "I finally got in," while someone with an ordinary result may feel that there is little worth sharing. A person who failed may not want to say anything at all.

This does not mean that every successful person posts or that every unsuccessful person disappears. It means that the probability of becoming visible may already depend on what happened. A success is often easier to announce than a failure. An achievement is easier to turn into a story than an unfinished attempt. A polished outcome is easier to share than a confusing process that is still underway.

Research on social-media participation consistently finds that a large share of users are mostly silent consumers, while a small minority produces a disproportionate amount of public content. Research on self-presentation reaches a similar conclusion: people tend to curate more positive, attractive, successful, or idealized versions of their lives. We still do not have a clean estimate for many everyday selection functions, such as how much more likely someone is to post after winning a competition than after losing one. Those probabilities vary by platform and context, but the direction is difficult to ignore.

The internet begins with a population that is already missing people in a non-random way. People who fail may be more likely to remain silent. Ordinary events may be less likely to be published. An unsuccessful experiment may stay in a notebook. A normal relationship may never become a story. The things missing from the feed are not random omissions; they disappear partly because they are ordinary, painful, private, or difficult to narrate.

## Selection compounds

There is another filter before recommendation begins: what feels worth saying. A person may live through hundreds of events in a year, but only a few become public stories. A promotion, a prize, a beautiful trip, a dramatic argument, or an unusually difficult experience is easier to publish than a normal Tuesday. Some people then post once a month, while others post ten times a day. Even among people with similar lives, the most active users contribute far more of the world that everyone else can see.

Consider two posts. One says, "I studied for eight hours today." The other says, "After failing twice, I finally got into Peking University." The second post has a stronger narrative, a clearer emotional arc, and a more visible outcome. It is easier to understand and easier to react to. Conflict, outrage, surprise, fear, accusation, and extreme success have the same advantage: they are salient.

This does not mean that users genuinely prefer everything they engage with. People may spend more time on angry political content without wanting more anger in their lives. They may comment because something is provocative, not because it is useful or representative. But when engagement becomes a ranking signal, attention advantage can become exposure advantage. A post that receives more reactions is given more opportunities to receive reactions.

Large audits and randomized feed experiments have found that engagement-oriented ranking can increase exposure to anger, anxiety, hostility, toxicity, and political conflict. One [study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118) found that out-group animosity is a strong driver of engagement, while a [field experiment](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adu5584) found that reranking partisan animosity altered affective polarization. The platform does not need to fabricate anything. It only needs to repeatedly amplify content that already has a selection advantage.

In a simple toy simulation, a group that represents only 1% of the underlying population can become roughly 8% of the feed under engagement-based ranking. If that group also posts ten times more actively than everyone else, its feed share can approach 50%. That is not a forecast of any particular platform. It is a reminder that small differences in visibility can become large differences in perceived reality.

## Everyone lives in a different neighborhood

Personalization adds another layer. If I spend my time reading about AI, research, competitions, graduate school, or startups, the system learns that these topics are likely to hold my attention. It begins showing me more of what people in that interest neighborhood are discussing, producing, and reacting to.

My feed becomes increasingly conditioned on my history, my network, my interests, and the behavior of people similar to me. An AI researcher opens Twitter and wonders why everyone is building agents. A student preparing for an exam opens Xiaohongshu and wonders why everyone is getting admitted. A founder checks their feed and wonders how everyone has already raised money.

Each person is living inside a different local reality. This does not mean that personalization automatically produces deep ideological polarization. Large experiments have found that changing what people see can alter exposure, while having mixed effects on more durable attitudes. Personalization clearly changes observation; its consequences for long-term belief are more complicated.

The emotional force of the system becomes clearest when we consider people who do not post. Suppose 100 people take an exam. Twenty succeed and 80 fail. Many of the successful students share their results. Most of the unsuccessful students do not. The feed now contains far more success stories than the underlying population does, and the platform distributes those stories because they attract attention.

The people who failed then encounter a stream dominated by the people who succeeded. They begin to think, "How did everyone else make it?" Many people like them may be on the other side of the screen, silently watching for exactly the same reason. A large number of silent failures are collectively consuming a small number of highly visible successes. Each success story may be completely real; the distortion lies in the implied frequency.

## The feed selects; the mind builds a model

The feed selects what we see. The mind turns what we see into a model of the world.

Humans naturally use what is easy to recall as a clue about what is common. Vivid, emotional, and repeatedly encountered information can feel more representative than it really is. We quietly substitute "I see this often" for "this happens often," even though seeing many admitted students tells us nothing about the admission rate, just as seeing many founders announce funding tells us nothing about how many startups failed quietly.

Research on social-media outrage has shown that observers tend to overestimate how angry other users actually are, and that this error can expand into mistaken beliefs about collective emotion, communication norms, hostility, and polarization ([Brady et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01582-0)). Other experiments have found that repeatedly seeing idealized or highly successful people can produce upward social comparison, lowering self-evaluation and well-being ([McComb et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2023.2180647)).

Put together, the process looks something like this: **world → published world → ranked world → personalized world → remembered world → perceived world.** Several links in this chain have been studied directly. We have evidence for unequal participation, selective self-presentation, engagement-based exposure, personalized information environments, overestimation of online outrage, and the psychological cost of repeated upward comparison.

What has not been established as clearly is the complete pipeline connecting them. How does an event that occurs in 1% of the population become 8%, 20%, or 50% of a feed? How does that altered feed change a person’s estimate of what is normal? How does that estimate influence later behavior and future posting? I am not claiming that existing research has already proved one unified theory of observation distortion. The existing evidence supports many of the links. The broader pipeline is a hypothesis about what happens when those links operate together.

That is why the important distinction is not simply true versus false, but representative versus selected. The successful student really did get admitted, the researcher really did publish, and the founder really did raise money. The missing context is the population from which those stories were selected. The internet can mislead us without showing us false information; it can show us true information that has been filtered so many times that the final composition no longer resembles the world it came from.

## What should change?

The answer is not to ban emotional content or force every feed into a perfect 50/50 balance. Extreme experiences are real, and people should be allowed to talk about them. Recommendation systems could still optimize for utility while accounting for whether repeated exposure is likely to distort a user’s understanding of the world. The aim would be to stop content with unusually strong attention advantages from being amplified without limit, not to remove unusual or controversial experiences.

Platforms could also provide more information about how a sample was formed. Instead of only saying, "You are seeing this because you watched AI content," a system might say, "Twenty-five percent of your recent feed belongs to this category. This is a personalized sample and does not represent its prevalence in the wider population." That would not remove selection bias, but it would make the selection visible.

We can develop a similar habit ourselves. When we see another announcement of a major achievement, we can ask why we are seeing so many of them before drawing a conclusion. I saw many does not mean there are many. It means that some process produced this observation, and that process may be worth examining.

The internet’s greatest danger may not be that it gives us false information. It may be that it gives us information that is true, but profoundly unrepresentative. A thousand true, carefully selected facts can still assemble a world that does not exist. The problem is not that we failed to see everyone. The problem is that the people we did not see were not missing at random. The feed is not the world. It is a selected observation of the world, and our minds are still learning how to tell the difference.
